Where should the money go?

You're buying a house. Every dollar you have can go into the down payment, into buying down the rate, or stay invested. This shows which, and when it stops being true.

2Put more down, or less?

Every extra dollar in the house retires a dollar of debt at your note rate, guaranteed. The same dollar in the market earns whatever the market gives.

Lenders often price a better rate once you cross 25% or 30% down. If your rate sheet shows that, put the better rate here. Otherwise leave it equal.

PMI runs 0.3% to 1.2% a year depending on credit score and how far under 20% you are. It cancels automatically at 78% of the original price, or at 80% if you ask. Since 2026 it is tax-deductible like mortgage interest, and the model treats it that way.

4What if rates move?

You hold a free option to refinance when rates fall. Points are a bet that you won't use it. A lender credit is a bet that you will.

The market rate steps to each value in the year listed and holds there. Each loan refinances the first month the market is far enough below its own note rate, into the same remaining term.

How this works

Every option is given the same cash at closing and the same monthly budget, set to the most expensive option's payment. Whatever an option doesn't spend at closing is invested. Whatever it doesn't spend each month is invested too, along with the tax benefit on that month's interest. At any point in time, wealth is the brokerage account minus the loan balance. The house is the same house in every option, so its value, its appreciation, its taxes and its insurance all cancel and never appear.

Under 20% down, PMI is charged on the original loan until the balance falls below the cancellation level, is paid out of the same budget, and gets the same tax benefit as interest, which it has had since the 2026 tax year. It is the one carrying cost that genuinely differs between down payments, which is why it is here and insurance and property tax are not.

That is why there is no "how much cash do you have" question. A point costs a percentage of the loan and saves a percentage of the loan, so loan size cancels. A dollar of down payment retires a dollar of debt at the note rate regardless of the house. Cash on hand decides which options you can afford, not which one is right.

Points and down payments are guaranteed returns. Investments are not. Everything here compares expected values and is blind to risk, so over long horizons it leans toward the market by construction. A lower payment is real insurance against a bad year and shows up nowhere in these numbers.

Discount points on a purchase are usually deductible in the year paid if you itemize; this doesn't credit that, so it is mildly harsh on buydowns. The tax benefit rate is held constant when it really falls as interest declines, which is mildly generous to them. Refinancing here keeps the remaining term rather than resetting to a fresh thirty years.
